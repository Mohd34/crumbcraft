The Thermodynamics of Strike Water & Friction Factor

In artisan bread baking, temperature is an ingredient just as critical as flour or water. Yeast cells (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) and lactic acid bacteria (Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis) are living biological organisms governed by biochemical reaction kinetics.

The Arrhenius Effect in Sourdough

According to the Arrhenius equation and the $Q_{10}$ temperature coefficient, enzymatic reaction rates roughly double with every 10°C (18°F) increase in dough temperature.

  • Below 21°C (70°F): Fermentation slows dramatically; yeast activity declines faster than bacterial acid production, resulting in sluggish rise and acidic, gummy crumb.
  • Between 24°C and 26°C (75°F to 78°F): The sweet spot for artisan sourdough. Optimal balance between yeast gas production (carbon dioxide) and gentle lactic acid development.
  • Above 29°C (84°F): Bacterial acidification accelerates rapidly. Proteolytic enzymes begin breaking down the gluten matrix, turning your dough into a soupy, unshapeable mass.

Why Ice Water Math Requires Thermodynamics

Many online baking guides mistakenly suggest simply mixing cold water with ice cubes by eye. However, water requires only 1 calorie per gram to change temperature by 1°C, but ice requires 80 calories per gram (latent heat of fusion) merely to melt from solid to liquid at 0°C without warming up at all!

CrumbCraft solves this exact thermodynamic equilibrium using the physical formula:

Mass_Ice = Mass_Water × (Temp_Tap − Temp_Target) ÷ (Temp_Tap + 80)

This guarantees that when the ice melts completely in your mixing bowl, your strike water will reach the exact target temperature without chilling your dough below fermentation threshold.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I determine my mixer's friction factor? +
To measure your mixer's friction factor accurately: measure your room, flour, and water temperature before mixing. Mix the dough for your normal duration (e.g. 5 minutes on speed 2). Take the dough temperature immediately after mixing. Calculate: Friction = (3 × Actual Dough Temp) − (Room Temp + Flour Temp + Water Temp). Save that number for all future bakes with that mixer!
Should I use the 3-factor or 4-factor formula? +
Use the 4-factor formula whenever you are using a preferment (sourdough starter, poolish, or biga) that comprises 15% or more of your recipe flour. Because the starter has significant mass, its temperature noticeably impacts the final mixture. Use the 3-factor formula for direct doughs using commercial active dry or instant yeast with no preferment.
