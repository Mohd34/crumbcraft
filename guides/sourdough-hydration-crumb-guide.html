Sourdough Hydration & Crumb Architecture Guide

Does higher hydration automatically mean better bread? Explore the physical mechanics of water, protein absorption, and crumb alveolation from 60% up to 88% hydration.

The Myth of "The Higher the Hydration, The Better"

In recent years, high hydration (80%+ sourdough) has become an internet obsession. However, pushing hydration beyond what your specific flour can absorb results in a soggy, gummy interior, weak scoring ears, and bread that collapses into a puddle on the peel.

The purpose of water is to hydrate the glutenin and gliadin proteins so they form an elastic network, and to gelatinize starch granules during baking. Water in excess of what the protein matrix can structurally bind simply weakens the dough.

Hydration Brackets & How to Handle Them

1. Moderate Hydration (65% – 70%)

Crumb Profile: Tight to medium-open, uniform crumb with small to medium air pockets.
Handling: Extremely easy to handle. Holds surface tension during shaping, easily scores with intricate leaf patterns, and yields a sturdy sandwich crumb that won't let butter drip through.

2. Classic Artisan Hydration (72% – 76%)

Crumb Profile: Balanced, open, custardy crumb with glossy interior walls and crisp blistered crust.
Handling: The sweet spot for artisan country boules and batards. Requires 3 to 4 sets of stretch-and-folds during the first 2 hours of bulk fermentation to organize the gluten sheets.

3. High Hydration (78% – 85%)

Crumb Profile: Wild, irregular, custardy alveoli with thin, translucent cell walls.
Handling: Slack and extensible. Requires strong roller-milled bread flour (12.7%+ protein). Bassinage (holding back 5% of the water and adding it in small splashes during mixing) is strongly recommended to build initial gluten strength before saturating the dough.
