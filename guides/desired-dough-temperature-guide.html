Thermal Physics in the Bakeshop

The Complete Guide to Desired Dough Temperature (DDT)

Learn how temperature dictates fermentation velocity, how to measure your mixer's mechanical friction factor, and how to compute exact strike water temperatures.

Why Temperature Overrules Clock Time

In baking instructions, you often read: "Let dough rise for 4 to 5 hours until doubled." But if your kitchen is 68°F (20°C) in winter, that dough might take 9 hours. In summer at 82°F (28°C), that same dough will turn into an overproofed pool of acid in 3.5 hours.

By calculating your water temperature before mixing, you hit your Desired Dough Temperature (typically 75°F to 78°F / 24°C to 26°C), allowing you to follow consistent, repeatable production schedules every single week regardless of outdoor weather.

The Sourdough 4-Factor Strike Formula

Because sourdough starter or levain represents a substantial thermal mass (often 20% of the flour weight), we use the 4-factor formula:

Strike Water Temp = (4 × Desired Dough Temp) − (Room Temp + Flour Temp + Starter Temp + Mixer Friction)

How to Calculate Mixer Friction Factor ($F$)

Mixers do work on the dough. That mechanical shear is converted directly into thermal heat energy. Hand kneading adds virtually no heat (~1°C / 2°F), whereas a KitchenAid stand mixer running on speed 2 for 8 minutes can add 8°C to 11°C (15°F to 20°F) of friction heat!

To calculate your mixer's exact friction factor:

  1. Measure the exact temperature of your Room ($T_R$), Flour ($T_F$), and Water ($T_W$) before mixing.
  2. Mix the dough for your normal duration (e.g., 6 minutes).
  3. Immediately probe the internal center of the mixed dough with an instant-read digital thermometer to find the Actual Dough Temp ($T_A$).
  4. Apply the friction formula: Friction = (3 × T_A) − (T_R + T_F + T_W).
